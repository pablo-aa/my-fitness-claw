# Adherence

**Bottom line:** The program that gets done for years beats the optimal one that gets dropped in two months.
Small doses of hard, progressive training on the big movement patterns deliver most of the result, so start small and add only what the person will sustain.
**Evidence:** moderate: minimal-dose effectiveness is well supported by meta-analyses and large datasets; direct trials on what makes people stick with lifting are scarce.

## Defaults

| pupil | grinder | maxxer |
|---|---|---|
| 2 full-body sessions/week, 30-45 min | 3 sessions/week, 45-60 min | 4-6 sessions/week, as long as it stays sustainable |
| 5-6 exercises: a squat or leg press, a hinge, an upper push, an upper pull, plus 1-2 extras | Full body or upper/lower, chosen by preference | Any split the person enjoys and can repeat |
| 1-2 hard sets per exercise | Enough sets to reach the [volume](volume.md) default | Volume set by individual response |
| Exercises the person likes and can do with confidence | Swap any exercise the person hates for an equivalent one | Same rule: no exercise is mandatory |

## Why the minimum works

Resistance training of almost any reasonable design beats no training for strength and size (Currier 2023, Currier 2026).
In a dataset of 14,690 adults (mean age 48) training once a week with single sets to failure on six exercises, strength rose about 30-50% in the first year (Steele 2023).
In resistance-trained men, a single set 2-3 times per week produced suboptimal but significant squat and bench press gains (Androulakis-Korakakis 2020).
A time-efficiency review recommends at least 4 weekly sets per muscle, built on one leg press or squat pattern, one upper pull and one upper push (Iversen 2021).
In older adults, low-volume programs were the most effective for most physical function tests (timed up and go, 6-min walk), lean mass and muscle size; higher volumes mattered mainly for strength (Radaelli 2025).
In cohort studies, the largest reduction in all-cause mortality appeared at roughly 30-60 min/week of muscle-strengthening activity (Momma 2022).

## What helps people stick with it

- Preference is a valid tiebreaker: split vs full body (Ramos-Campo 2024), training frequency at equal volume (Schoenfeld 2019) and free weights vs machines (Haugen 2023) do not change results meaningfully.
- Training to failure feels worse than stopping 1-2 reps short, with no clear hypertrophy advantage (Refalo 2025, Refalo 2023).
- Advanced techniques (drop sets, clusters, etc.) are not better than traditional sets, so they are optional, not required (Cowley 2026).
- Breaks are not catastrophic: after a 10-week pause, untrained adults regained strength and size quickly and ended up level with continuous trainers (Halonen 2024).
- ACSM calls adherence "of paramount importance" and recommends individualizing for enjoyment and safety (Currier 2026).

## When to go beyond the default

Add sessions, sets or exercises only when the person has trained consistently for several weeks and wants more.
More volume does produce more hypertrophy, with diminishing returns (see [volume](volume.md)), so the extra is real but smaller than the first dose.

## Caveats

- The Steele 2023 data are retrospective records from one company, not a randomized trial.
- Momma 2022 is observational; the J-shaped curve above 60 min/week is uncertain and should not be read as "more is harmful".
- Evidence on behavioral strategies specific to lifting adherence is thin; much of the advice here is inferred from equivalence findings (if two options work equally, pick the preferred one).
