# Energy balance

**Bottom line:** Calories decide whether weight goes down, stays or goes up; equations only give a starting guess, and the weekly average of morning weigh-ins tells you the real maintenance.
Lose about 0.5-1% of body weight per week and gain about 0.25-0.5% per week; faster mostly costs muscle on the way down and adds fat on the way up.
**Evidence:** strong that energy balance drives weight change; moderate for the specific rates (a few RCTs plus position stands); moderate for the size of metabolic adaptation.

## Defaults

| pupil | grinder | maxxer |
|---|---|---|
| No kcal number; to lose, shrink the starch quarter of the plate and drop liquid calories | Estimate maintenance once, then set an approximate target | Estimate maintenance, then correct it from 2-3 weeks of logged intake and weight trend |
| Weigh a few mornings a week, judge the trend monthly | Fat loss: ~20% below maintenance, aim 0.5-1%/week | Fat loss: 0.5-1%/week, slower (~0.5-0.7%) when already lean |
| Muscle gain: eat a bit more at meals, aim for a slow scale increase | Muscle gain: ~10% above maintenance, aim 0.25-0.5%/week | Muscle gain: 5-10% surplus; advanced lifters ~0.25%/week or less |
| | Adjust when the weekly average is off target for 2-3 weeks | Adjust kcal in small steps (practitioner heuristic: ~5-10%) when the 7-day average misses the target for 2-3 weeks |

## Estimating maintenance

Mifflin-St Jeor is the most reliable common equation for resting metabolic rate, predicting within 10% of measured for more people than other equations, but individual errors are still noteworthy (Frankenfield 2005).
Activity multipliers on top of that add more error, so treat any total as a starting guess.
Real maintenance is the intake at which the weekly average weight holds steady.
Body weight responds slowly to a sustained intake change (half time of about a year) because expenditure falls as weight falls, so expect the rate of loss to slow over a long diet (Hall 2011).

## Deficit size and lean mass

Natural bodybuilding recommendations target 0.5-1% of body weight per week to maximize muscle retention (Helms 2014).
In elite athletes who lifted 4x/week, losing ~0.7%/week (19% energy cut) increased lean mass by 2.1%, while a faster plan (target 1.4%/week, achieved ~1.0%, 30% cut) left it unchanged (Garthe 2011).
A meta-analysis found an energy deficit impairs lean mass gains from resistance training but not strength gains, and recommends avoiding deficits larger than ~500 kcal/day when the goal is to keep muscle (Murphy 2022).
The higher the starting body fat, the more aggressive the deficit can be; leaner people do better with slower loss (Aragon 2017).

## Surplus size

Off-season recommendations are a ~10-20% surplus and ~0.25-0.5% body weight gain per week for novice and intermediate lifters, with advanced lifters more conservative (Iraki 2019).
In trained lifters, a 15% surplus increased skinfolds more than maintenance, while muscle thickness and squat strength did not differ meaningfully between maintenance, 5% and 15% surpluses; bench press 1RM did rise more with the 15% surplus (Helms 2023).
The surplus that maximizes muscle relative to fat has never been pinned down (Slater 2019).

## Metabolic adaptation, honestly sized

Energy expenditure drops during dieting beyond what the lost tissue explains, but in ordinary dieting the extra drop is small.
After an 8-week 1,000 kcal/day diet, resting metabolic rate was about 92 kcal/day lower than predicted, the gap halved after weight stabilized, and it did not predict regain (Martins 2020).
The famous large numbers come from extreme cases: Biggest Loser contestants still showed about -499 kcal/day of adaptation 6 years later after losing ~58 kg in 30 weeks (Fothergill 2016).
Adaptation is real and attenuates weight loss; the athlete-focused review recommends small deficits and a slow rate of loss, and notes that refeeds and diet breaks are still untested (Trexler 2014).

## When to go beyond the default

Track intake precisely when the weight trend stays flat despite reported adherence, before contest or photo-shoot prep, or for lean, advanced lifters chasing slow gains where errors of 100-200 kcal/day matter.

## Caveats

- Rate-of-loss data in lean lifters come from small trials (Garthe 2011, n = 24) and narrative reviews.
- Helms 2023 had only 17 completers; it supports small surpluses but does not settle the optimal size.
- Day-to-day weight swings from water, salt, carbs and gut content are large; read only multi-week averages.
- Not medical advice: aggressive deficits, very low calorie diets, pregnancy, adolescents and anyone with an eating disorder history need professional supervision.
