# Carbs and fat

**Bottom line:** Once calories and protein are set, the carb-to-fat split is mostly personal preference.
Keep fat from going very low, and keep enough carbs to train well if sessions are long or high volume.
**Evidence:** strong that low-carb and low-fat diets give similar fat loss at equal calories and protein; moderate for a fat floor; moderate that carbs barely affect typical lifting sessions.

## Defaults

| pupil | grinder | maxxer |
|---|---|---|
| No split to track; the plate already gives a starch quarter and some fat | Fill remaining calories after protein with whatever carb/fat mix the person prefers | Fat 0.5-1.5 g/kg/day (or ~15-30% of kcal), rest from carbs |
| Some starch or fruit in the meal before training | Avoid very low fat for long periods | Carbs ≥3-5 g/kg/day when bulking or training high volume, if preferred |
| | | Low-carb or keto is fine if the person prefers it and performance holds |

## Low-carb vs low-fat

At equal calories, cutting carbs does not give a metabolic advantage: in a meta-analysis of 32 controlled feeding studies, energy expenditure (26 kcal/day) and fat loss (16 g/day) were slightly greater with lower-fat diets: tiny differences, and in the opposite direction from the low-carb claim (Hall & Guo 2017).
In a metabolic ward, cutting fat produced more body fat loss than cutting carbs by the same calories (89 vs 53 g/day over 6 days) (Hall 2015).
In free-living adults over 12 months, healthy low-fat and healthy low-carb diets produced the same weight loss (Gardner 2018), and named diets of both kinds landed close together (Johnston 2014).
The ISSN concludes that approaches from low-fat to ketogenic can be similarly effective for body composition (Aragon 2017).

## Minimum fat

Contest-prep recommendations put fat at 15-30% of calories (Helms 2014), and off-season recommendations at 0.5-1.5 g/kg/day (Iraki 2019).
In men, low-fat diets lowered total testosterone modestly versus higher-fat diets (standardized mean difference -0.38, 95% CI -0.75 to -0.01, high heterogeneity) in a meta-analysis of 6 small crossover studies (206 men) (Whittaker & Wu 2021).
Whether that drop affects muscle gain is unknown, but there is no benefit to going below the floor.

## Carbs for training performance

A systematic review of 49 studies found carbohydrate intake is unlikely to change strength training performance in a fed state for workouts up to about 10 sets per muscle group; higher-volume sessions may benefit (Henselmans 2022).
Off-season bodybuilding recommendations still suggest ≥3-5 g/kg/day of carbs to support training demands (Iraki 2019).
Endurance athletes are a different case and need much more carbohydrate (Kerksick 2017).

## When to go beyond the default

Raise carbs for long, high-volume sessions, twice-a-day training or concurrent endurance work.
Go low-carb only if the person prefers it, finds it easier to control appetite, and training quality holds.

## Caveats

- Experts still disagree on the carbohydrate-insulin model of obesity; its proponents argue diet composition drives intake and fat storage (Ludwig 2021), while controlled feeding data do not show a fat loss advantage for low carb at equal calories (Hall & Guo 2017).
- The testosterone data come from 6 small studies and the clinical meaning is uncertain.
- A 2026 corrigendum (doi:10.1016/j.jsbmb.2025.106880) leaves the results unchanged but corrects the methods: full-text screening and one risk-of-bias domain were done by the first author alone, not independently by both, and further post hoc subgroup analyses (ethnicity, study quality) were run but not reported; this lowers confidence in the review (content read from the authors' corrected arXiv version, arXiv:2204.00007v3, as the publisher page was not accessible).
- Diet quality (fiber, whole foods, fruit and vegetables) still matters for health even when the split does not matter for body composition.
- Not medical advice: diabetes, especially on insulin or sulfonylureas, requires medical guidance before any carbohydrate change.
