# Protein

**Bottom line:** About 1.6 g/kg/day captures most of the muscle-building benefit for people who lift; going up to 2.2 g/kg is cheap insurance, and more helps mainly when lean and dieting.
Spread it over 3-5 protein-containing meals; source (animal or plant) matters little once the total is adequate.
**Evidence:** strong for adding protein to resistance training (several large meta-analyses); moderate for the exact plateau; moderate for higher needs in a deficit; moderate for per-meal distribution.

## Defaults

| pupil | grinder | maxxer |
|---|---|---|
| A palm-sized protein portion (meat, fish, eggs, dairy, tofu, legumes) at every meal | 1.6 g/kg/day, as 3-4 meals with a solid protein portion | 1.6-2.2 g/kg/day |
| Protein shake only if meals fall short | Round up toward 2 g/kg in a deficit | Lean and dieting: 2.3-3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass |
| | | ~0.4 g/kg per meal across at least 4 meals |

## Daily target and plateau

In 49 RCTs (1,863 participants), protein supplementation increased resistance training gains in strength and fat-free mass; gains plateaued at about 1.62 g/kg/day (95% CI 1.03-2.20) (Morton 2018).
The upper end of that interval is why 2.2 g/kg is a sensible ceiling for people who want to be sure.
A 105-RCT dose-response meta-analysis found lean mass rose with each extra 0.1 g/kg/day, steeply up to 1.3 g/kg and more gently above it (Tagawa 2021).
A strength dose-response meta-analysis found strength gains with training rose with protein up to about 1.5 g/kg/day and not beyond (Tagawa 2022).
In 74 RCTs, extra protein produced small additional lean mass gains in people doing resistance exercise, significant in under-65s at 1.6 g/kg/day or more (Nunes 2022).
The ISSN position is 1.4-2.0 g/kg/day for most exercising people (Jäger 2017).

## During a deficit

Protein needs rise with the size of the deficit and with leanness: 2.3-3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass for lean, resistance-trained people dieting (Helms 2014 review, Helms 2014 recommendations).
The ISSN diets position stand reaches the same range (Aragon 2017).
Note the unit: this is per kg of fat-free mass, so at 15% body fat it is roughly 2.0-2.6 g/kg of body weight.

## Per-meal distribution

Evenly spaced doses of 20-40 g (about 0.25-0.40 g/kg) every 3-4 hours are the ISSN recommendation (Kerksick 2017).
A review of acute and long-term data suggests ~0.4 g/kg per meal over at least 4 meals, up to ~0.55 g/kg per meal at 2.2 g/kg/day (Schoenfeld 2018).
The old "only 25-30 g per meal can be used" idea is wrong: 100 g of protein after exercise produced a larger and longer (>12 h) anabolic response than 25 g (Trommelen 2023), and 70 g beat 40 g for whole-body net balance (Kim 2016).
So distribution is a fine-tuning lever; the daily total matters far more.

## Plant protein

Across 16 RCTs, protein source did not change absolute lean mass or strength, with a small advantage for animal protein in percent lean mass and in adults under 50 (Lim 2021).
A 2026 meta-analysis of 18 RCTs lasting at least 6 months found no differences in lean mass or strength between plant (mostly soy) and animal protein supplements (Yimam 2026).
Habitual vegans at 1.6 g/kg/day (whole foods plus soy isolate) gained muscle and strength like protein-matched omnivores over 12 weeks (Hevia-Larraín 2021).
Practical rule for plant eaters: hit the target, mix sources, and lean toward the upper end of the range.

## Safety

High-protein diets (≥1.5 g/kg or ≥100 g/day) did not change kidney function over time in healthy adults across 28 trials (Devries 2018).

## When to go beyond the default

Go to 2.2 g/kg/day or the fat-free-mass based deficit range when lean and in a prolonged deficit, when the person is plant-based, or simply when higher protein helps them feel full.

## Caveats

- The benefit of extra protein shrinks with age and is larger in already-trained lifters (Morton 2018).
- The plateau estimate is a population average with a wide confidence interval, and the break point itself was not statistically significant (p = 0.079); individuals vary.
- Not medical advice: people with kidney disease must not raise protein without their doctor.
