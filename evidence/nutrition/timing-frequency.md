# Meal timing and frequency

**Bottom line:** Eat the number of meals that fits the person's life; with total calories and protein equal, meal frequency, breakfast and intermittent fasting barely change fat loss or muscle.
There is no narrow "anabolic window": a protein-containing meal within a few hours before or after training covers it.
**Evidence:** strong that meal frequency and protein timing matter little when totals are equal; moderate for intermittent fasting with lifting (short trials); moderate for diet breaks (few RCTs, mixed results).

## Defaults

| pupil | grinder | maxxer |
|---|---|---|
| 3 meals (or whatever pattern already works), each with protein | 3-4 meals with protein spread through the day | 4+ protein feedings of ~0.4 g/kg, every 3-4 h, if convenient |
| Eat a normal meal within a few hours of training | A protein meal within a few hours before or after training | Protein meal 1-2 h pre and post training, pre-sleep protein optional |
| Breakfast optional | Intermittent fasting is fine if preferred | Planned diet breaks at maintenance for long diets, if useful for adherence |

## Meal frequency

A meta-analysis of 15 studies found an apparent benefit of more frequent meals for body composition, but it was driven by a single study; the authors advise against reading it as real (Schoenfeld 2015).
Whole-body calorimetry and doubly-labelled water studies show no difference in 24 h energy expenditure between nibbling and gorging (Bellisle 1997).
In non-exercising settings, the ISSN concludes meal frequency has limited impact on weight loss and body composition, with stronger evidence for effects on appetite (Kerksick 2017).
Skipping breakfast does not cause weight gain: in RCTs, breakfast eaters ate about 260 kcal/day more and weighed slightly more (0.44 kg) than skippers (Sievert 2019).

## Protein timing and the anabolic window

When total protein intake was controlled for, protein timing around training had no significant effect on strength (20 studies) or hypertrophy (23 studies); total protein intake was the strongest predictor of hypertrophy (Schoenfeld 2013).
The anabolic effect of exercise lasts at least 24 h, so timing is a matter of tolerance and convenience (Jäger 2017).
Protein doses of 20-40 g every 3-4 h and ~30-40 g of casein before sleep are reasonable refinements for people who want them (Kerksick 2017).
Off-season bodybuilding recommendations include protein meals within 1-2 h before and after training (Iraki 2019).

## Intermittent fasting

In 8 studies of people who lifted, intermittent fasting reduced body mass and fat versus control diets, with similar fat-free mass retention (Ashtary-Larky 2021).
Part of that effect is likely just eating less: when calories were matched over 12 months, time-restricted eating plus calorie restriction did not beat calorie restriction alone (8.0 vs 6.3 kg, non-significant) (Liu 2022).
The ISSN finds no body composition advantage of intermittent over daily calorie restriction (Aragon 2017).
Use it as an appetite-control tool, not a metabolic trick.

## Diet breaks and refeeds

In men with obesity, alternating 2 weeks of dieting with 2 weeks at maintenance produced more weight and fat loss than continuous dieting (14.1 vs 9.1 kg weight loss), at the cost of a longer total timeline (Byrne 2018).
In resistance-trained adults, 1-week diet breaks every 3 weeks gave the same fat loss and fat-free mass retention as continuous dieting, but with less hunger (Peos 2021).
So diet breaks are a sustainability tool for long diets, not a fat loss accelerator.
Short refeed days (1-2 days) have little direct evidence.

## When to go beyond the default

Pin down meal timing and frequency only for contest prep, very high training volumes or twice-daily sessions, where glycogen recovery between sessions matters (Kerksick 2017).

## Caveats

- Most meal frequency and breakfast studies are short and many have high risk of bias.
- Intermittent fasting trials with lifting are short (weeks) and small.
- MATADOR was in men with obesity; ICECAP in trained adults; results may not transfer between groups.
- Not medical advice: fasting protocols are not appropriate for people with diabetes on glucose-lowering drugs, pregnancy, or an eating disorder history without professional guidance.
