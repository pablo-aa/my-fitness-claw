# Adherence

**Bottom line:** The diet that works is the one the person keeps eating; low-carb, low-fat and every named diet in between land in the same place when calories and protein are similar.
Start with the simplest structure that controls portions and protein, and add counting only when the person wants it or progress stalls.
**Evidence:** strong that diet type matters little for weight loss (large RCTs and network meta-analyses); moderate that flexible beats rigid restraint (mostly cross-sectional); weak to moderate for plate and hand-portion tools.

## Defaults

| pupil | grinder | maxxer |
|---|---|---|
| No counting: plate method (half vegetables or fruit, a quarter protein, a quarter starch) | Plate method plus a daily protein target and a rough kcal target | Full macro tracking if the person enjoys it |
| A palm-sized protein portion at every meal | Track for 1-2 weeks to calibrate portions, then loosen | Weekly adjustments from the weight trend (see [energy balance](energy-balance.md)) |
| Keep favorite foods in, in portions that fit | 80/20 flexible: no forbidden foods | Flexible restraint still applies: no food is "off plan" |
| Weigh in a few mornings a week | Weigh in most mornings, look at the weekly average | Daily weigh-in, 7-day average |

## Why diet type matters less than sticking to it

Across 48 RCTs (7,286 adults), low-carb and low-fat diets each produced about 7.3 kg loss at 12 months versus no diet, and differences between named diets were small; the authors conclude any diet a patient will adhere to is a valid choice (Johnston 2014).
In DIETFITS, 609 adults on a healthy low-fat or healthy low-carb diet lost 5.3 vs 6.0 kg at 12 months, a non-significant difference, and neither genotype nor insulin secretion predicted who did better (Gardner 2018).
Atkins, Zone, Weight Watchers and Ornish all produced modest 1-year loss; adherence was low overall, and within every diet better adherence meant more weight lost (Dansinger 2005).
The ISSN concludes that a wide range of approaches, from low-fat to ketogenic, can be similarly effective for body composition, and that long-term success depends on compliance (Aragon 2017).

## Flexible vs rigid restraint

Flexible dieting (moderate, no all-or-nothing rules) is associated with less overeating, lower body mass and less depression and anxiety; calorie counting and conscious dieting are associated with overeating alone and higher body mass (Smith 1999).
Food cravings fully mediated the link between rigid control and poor dieting success, while flexible control predicted success independently (Meule 2011).
In resistance-trained adults on a ~20% deficit, a flexible diet and a rigid fixed-food diet produced similar weight loss over 10 weeks (Conlin 2021).
So rigidity buys nothing for results and costs adherence and wellbeing.

## Simple structures for beginners

In 130 adults with obesity and type 2 diabetes, a portion-control plate produced more 6-month weight loss than usual dietary teaching (1.8% vs 0.1%) (Pedersen 2007).
A 2026 meta-analysis of 14 RCTs (1,762 adults with chronic disease) found early weight loss with portion-control plates (about 3.7 kg at 3 months) that faded by 6 months, with more durable glycemic and lipid benefits (Zhang 2026).
Hand-based portion estimates are reasonably accurate for geometric foods: with finger-width measures, 80% of estimates were within ±25% of true weight (Gibson 2016).
No trial has tested a hand-portion system as a weight loss intervention, so treat it as a practical estimation aid, not a proven method.
Regular self-weighing is associated with more weight loss and not with worse depression or anxiety in people seeking weight loss (Zheng 2015).

## When to go beyond the default

Move from plate and portions to counting when progress stalls for 3-4 weeks at stable adherence, when the goal needs precision (contest prep, lean bulk at an advanced level), or when the person simply likes numbers.
The 3-4 week trigger is a practical heuristic, not a tested threshold.
Counting is a tool, not a virtue: drop back to portions once the person can eyeball their targets.

## Caveats

- Most diet comparison trials are in adults with overweight or obesity, not lean lifters.
- Flexible vs rigid restraint data are mostly cross-sectional questionnaires; only one small RCT exists in lifters (Conlin 2021, n = 23).
- Plate-method trials are mostly in people with diabetes or chronic disease, and the weight effect fades over time.
- In a cross-sectional community sample, intuitive eating predicted less disordered eating and fewer body image concerns than rigid control, so it may suit people prone to disordered eating better than structured restraint (Linardon 2017).
- Not medical advice: anyone with an eating disorder history, diabetes on medication, kidney disease, or who is pregnant should plan their diet with a professional.
