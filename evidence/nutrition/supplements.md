# Supplements

**Bottom line:** Supplements are the top of the pyramid: small effects, only after calories, protein and training are in place.
Creatine monohydrate is the one worth taking for almost anyone who lifts; caffeine helps performance; protein powder is just convenient food; vitamin D only when a clinician finds a reason.
**Evidence:** strong for creatine and caffeine (position stands, many meta-analyses); strong that protein powder works like any protein; strong that most other products lack evidence.

## Creatine

Creatine increases muscle creatine stores and improves high-intensity performance and training adaptations; up to 30 g/day for 5 years has been safe in healthy people (Kreider 2017).
In a meta-analysis of 35 RCTs, creatine combined with resistance training added about 1.1 kg of lean body mass versus placebo, regardless of age; males gained more than females, whose gain was not significant (Delpino 2022).
3-5 g/day (or 0.1 g/kg/day) is the standard dose (Antonio 2021).
Loading (20-25 g/day for 5-7 days) is not required: 3 g/day reached similar muscle creatine levels after about 28 days, just more slowly (Antonio 2021).
The evidence does not support the common fears about kidney damage, hair loss, dehydration or cramping in healthy users; the hair loss idea comes from one unreplicated study showing a DHT rise (Antonio 2021).
Short-term water retention inside muscle is possible, mostly not seen over longer periods, and creatine does not increase fat mass (Antonio 2021).

## Caffeine

Caffeine at 3-6 mg/kg improves many forms of performance, including muscular endurance, strength and sprinting, with effects as low as 2 mg/kg possible and side effects common at 9 mg/kg (Guest 2021).
The usual timing is 60 min before exercise; performance response and side effects on sleep and anxiety vary between individuals (Guest 2021).

## Protein powder

Protein supplementation enhances muscle and strength gains with training, with no further fat-free mass gain beyond ~1.6 g/kg/day total protein (Morton 2018).
Whole foods can cover protein needs; powder is a practical way to hit the target (Jäger 2017).

## Vitamin D

The Endocrine Society suggests against supplementation above the dietary reference intake for healthy adults under 75, and against routine 25(OH)D testing without an established indication (Demay 2024).
So: correct a diagnosed deficiency, otherwise do not megadose.

## What is not worth it

- Only a few performance supplements have good evidence: caffeine, creatine, nitrate, sodium bicarbonate and possibly beta-alanine; responses vary and contamination with banned substances is a real risk (Maughan 2018).
- BCAAs: no human evidence that BCAAs alone stimulate muscle protein synthesis; complete protein already contains them (Wolfe 2017).
- Fat burners: apart from caffeine and green tea, evidence is lacking for popular ingredients like carnitine, CLA, chromium and forskolin (Jeukendrup & Randell 2011).
- Testosterone boosters: of 109 ingredients in 50 products, only 24.8% had any data showing a testosterone increase, and some products exceeded safe upper limits for vitamins and minerals (Clemesha 2020).

## When to go beyond the default

Beta-alanine (3-5 g/day) is an optional extra: the IOC lists it as possibly beneficial for sustained high-intensity efforts (Maughan 2018), and it appears in bodybuilding recommendations (Iraki 2019), but its benefit is smaller and more context-specific than creatine's.
Anything else needs a specific reason and a source that meets the bar in this knowledge base.

## Caveats

- Supplements are loosely regulated; choose third-party tested products, especially athletes subject to drug testing (Maughan 2018).
- Caffeine needs individual trial; people with heart conditions, anxiety disorders, pregnancy or sleep problems should limit it.
- Not medical advice: anyone with kidney disease, on medication, pregnant or under 18 should talk to a doctor before taking supplements.
